\section{Supplementary Note 1: Retrospective analyses and confirmatory design}

The historical experiments are retrospective. The analysis specifies the fitted-regime rule, the extrapolation-region fraction (0.30), the four forms \{power, exponential, linear, constant\}, precision floors and random seeds. The 0.5 threshold is the median calibration score and is used for screening; statistical rejection requires separate calibration. A confirmatory design fixes a primary threshold at a 5\% false-positive rate (FPR) and a secondary threshold at a 1\% FPR on an independent calibration set before scoring the test set. We report finite-sample upper-tail probabilities, count calibration ties conservatively and apply Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) false-discovery-rate control when multiple datasets are scanned. The analysis specifies the assignment rule, exclusion criteria, data transformations, observation models, boundary intervals and abstention rules.

Each test specifies \(\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{M},\Theta,P_\varepsilon,I,A,S,\alpha)\): admissible models, fitted parameters, the observation/censoring model, fitting data, the complete adaptive algorithm, the final statistic and the error rate. Direction choice, window search, model selection and parameter fitting belong to \(A\) and are repeated in every null or calibration replicate. The four verdicts are \emph{compatible} (the pre-horizon prediction fits over the measured range), \emph{localized incompatibility} (it fits a regime and fails beyond an estimated boundary), \emph{whole-range incompatibility} (the pre-horizon prediction is rejected over the sampled range and no compatible regime is identified) and \emph{abstention} (the inputs do not support a test). Incompatibility with an established theory requires an admissible model set that adequately encodes its relevant prediction; incompatibility with the four forms alone establishes departure only from those forms.

\section{Supplementary Note 2: Extrapolation-residual ratio and invariances}

For data $(x,y)$, the best of the four classical forms, $f$, is fitted in the fitted regime and extrapolated to the extrapolation region. With log-residuals $r(x)=\lvert\log(\lvert y\rvert/\lvert f(x)\rvert)\rvert$, the statistic is

\[
\text{extrapolation-residual ratio}=\frac{\operatorname{median}_{\text{extrapolation region}} r}{\operatorname{median}_{\text{fitted regime}} r}.
\]

In the invariance tests, the ratio is unchanged under $y\to a\,y$ (amplitude) and $x\to b\,x$ (units), and tolerates additive noise up to 5--10\%. The split-conformal nonconformity is the mean absolute residual in the extrapolation region divided by the 90th percentile of the residuals in the fitted regime, with the same one-percent relative floor. This scaled residual has no coverage guarantee of its own. The ratio orders datasets by departure from the fitted form. Reported magnitudes, such as the FIRAS over-prediction factor, are given with their window dependence and a bootstrap interval (Supplementary Note~5).

\section{Supplementary Note 4: Confidence intervals and false-positive rates}

The extrapolation screen reaches AUROC 0.80 with bootstrap 95\% confidence interval (CI) [0.64, 0.94], resampled over 36 synthetic phenomena. In the broader mechanism-level benchmark, an uncapped log score is calibrated separately at $n = 5$, 9, 43 and 120 using 785 valid classical simulations per size. Across 12 synthetic departure families with eight samples each, TPR is 0.271 at the calibration-targeted 5\% FPR (mechanism-bootstrap 95\% CI [0.073, 0.500]) and 0 at the targeted 1\% FPR. Smooth roll-off and discontinuous-drop simulations have sensitivity 1.0, whereas seven families have zero sensitivity. On a separate synthetic null set of 12 classical function families with eight samples each, the observed FPR is 0/96 at both thresholds; neither class has a fit failure or abstention. This null is synthetic and independent of the calibration set. The evaluation of formula-derived point clouds (200 formula point clouds and 30 synthetic departures) reaches AUROC 0.88, recall 0.90 and false-positive rate 0.24 at the median calibration threshold.

The historical-case values in this paragraph are tail probabilities of the extrapolation score, separate from the named-prediction tests of Supplementary Note~11. They use 392 valid scores from 400 classical calibration datasets. The primary statistic is capped at a maximum value; with this capped statistic, FIRAS, copper specific heat, Bertozzi and Onnes have upper-tail p-values 0.053, 0.053, 0.346 and 0.346, and BH-adjusted q-values 0.107, 0.107, 0.346 and 0.346. The cap is reached by approximately 5\% of full-size and 34\% of small-size classical calibration datasets, which limits the smallest attainable p-value. An uncapped log-combination, examined after the capped results were known, gives nominal p-values 0.031, 0.023, 0.176 and 0.173, none of which survives four-case BH control.

\section{Supplementary Note 5: Real-data details}

\begin{description}
\item[COBE-FIRAS.] The 43-point spectrum measured by the Cosmic Background Explorer (COBE) Far Infrared Absolute Spectrophotometer (FIRAS), published by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) on its Legacy Archive for Microwave Background Data Analysis (LAMBDA),
(\nolinkurl{firas_monopole_spec_v1.txt}; Fixsen et al.~1996) spans
$x = h\nu/kT \in [1.2, 11.3]$ at $T = 2.725$~K. It does not reach the pure
Rayleigh--Jeans regime ($x \ll 1$), and the classical anchor lies on the shoulder of the
peak at $\nu \approx 5.45$~cm$^{-1}$. The over-prediction factor varies from
${\approx}3008\times$ with a 10\% window to ${\approx}475\times$ with a 40\% window. At a
20\% window, the error-weighted Rayleigh--Jeans comparison using published $\sigma$ gives
bootstrap CI [1497, 2423]. The verdict is whole-range incompatibility; these observations do not identify a transition
boundary.
\item[Bertozzi 1964.] The five digitized published values (Am. J. Phys. 32, 551) are
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{KE}\ (\mathrm{MeV}) &= \{0.5,\ 1,\ 1.5,\ 4.5,\ 15\},\\
\beta^{2} = v^{2}/c^{2} &= \{0.752,\ 0.828,\ 0.922,\ 0.974,\ 1.0\}.
\end{align*}
Here KE denotes kinetic energy. The classical law
$\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$ gives $\beta^{2} = 2\cdot\mathrm{KE}/(m_ec^{2}) = 3.914\cdot\mathrm{KE}$
for $m_ec^{2} = 0.511$~MeV. It reaches $\beta^{2} = 58.7$ ($v = 7.7c$) at 15~MeV, whereas
the measurements saturate at $\beta^{2} = 1$, giving a ${\approx}59\times$ over-prediction.
The dataset contains no non-relativistic regime: its lowest point is
$\beta^{2} = 0.752$ ($v = 0.87c$, $\gamma = 2.0$). The verdict for the Newtonian prediction is whole-range
incompatibility; these observations do not localize its validity boundary.
\item[Solar-System Kepler.] In this non-circular control, $a$ and $P$ are measured
independently for eight planets. The fitted exponent is 1.500 and the descriptive verdict
is \emph{compatible} over the measured range. We exclude the NASA
Exoplanet Archive set because $a$ is often back-computed from $P$ through Kepler's law.
\end{description}

Fitting the extrapolation screen's high-temperature regime on each of the six NIST reference curves selects a power form in every case and gives fitted-regime $R^{2}$ from 0.965 to 0.998 and extrapolation-residual ratios from 127 to 1,490; the largest $R^{2}$ is beryllium's, whose rising high-temperature segment has no Dulong--Petit plateau. These statistics describe the selected power fits, not goodness of fit to the Dulong--Petit constant.

\section{Supplementary Note 6: Underdetermination of form and interpretation}

Recognition, form recovery and interpretation are distinct tasks. Recognition is incompatibility with a model set and observation model specified before testing. Departure from the four forms establishes incompatibility with those forms alone; it need not imply departure from classical physics. For form recovery, sufficiently rich function classes fit a finite dataset arbitrarily well on a bounded window, so the replacement law is underdetermined without a parsimony prior. With such a prior, symbolic regression can recover a candidate mathematical form (Guimer\`a et al., Sci. Adv. 2020; Udrescu \& Tegmark, Sci. Adv. 2020). Interpretation is not identifiable from data alone. In Planck's case, the claim that the fitted constant quantizes energy exchange is a theoretical commitment outside the data. Millikan's photoelectric line illustrates the distinction: its linear shape is admitted by the four forms, while its residuals reject a supplied classical energy prediction.

\section{Supplementary Note 7: Case studies and assignment rule}

The case studies combine real measurements, reference curves and synthetic formula evaluations; they are descriptive and do not estimate accuracy across mechanisms; the assignment rule is shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{sfig:routing}. Cases with a measured classical regime are assigned to the extrapolation screen. Cases without one require the named-prediction test and a pre-1900 prediction; cases with neither input receive an abstention verdict. FIRAS and Bertozzi lack a measured classical regime and require a named-prediction test. Specific heat and Onnes are candidates for extrapolation-screen evidence, subject to raw-data and censoring checks. Interference examples without a classical regime or a specified pre-1900 prediction are abstentions.

\section{Supplementary Note 8: Representation-screen encoders, controls and training-corpus provenance}

The representation screen uses two encoders. The large encoder is trained on reductions of pre-1900 laws, with training, calibration and test formulas separated by law family (Supplementary Fig.~3; Supplementary Note~16). The compact encoder, compared here with its controls, is a set transformer of width 128 with three induced-set-attention blocks, trained for 1,000 steps on two independently subsampled and rescaled copies of each of 205 pre-1900 point clouds from 40 families. Families are sampled without replacement so that repeated laws cannot become false contrastive negatives, and an auxiliary head predicts functional signatures, which record the elementary function types present in each formula. No synthetic departure, saturation, step, kink, splice or crossover example enters this optimization. We trained matched models with International System of Units (SI) dimension metadata and with zeroed dimension metadata; the model trained without unit information is the compact encoder reported throughout.

We compare the compact encoder with a generic-function control encoder trained on analytic functions without historical restrictions. It is matched to the compact encoder in dataset count (205), pseudo-family count (40), exact family-size profile, full histogram of eight-bit functional signatures (which record the elementary function types present in each formula), architecture, initialization seed and optimization schedule. Each bit of the eight-bit signature records whether one elementary function type (exponential, logarithm, sine, square root, power, inverse power, polynomial terms or hyperbolic) appears in the formula. Its globally valid analytic generators contain no piecewise failure or anomaly supervision. On 12 synthetic departure families and 12 classical families (Supplementary Fig.~4c), family AUROC is 0.375 [0.167, 0.618], versus 0.903 for the compact encoder trained on pre-1900 laws; the paired family-bootstrap difference is 0.528 [0.264, 0.764]. Its calibrated threshold detects 10/12 synthetic departure families but also flags 11/12 synthetic classical families and 4/7 held-out cited classical test families. Thus it is sensitive but non-specific. This single matched generic-function control supports an incremental role for the historical corpus.

We retrained both encoders from scratch under three paired initialization and augmentation seeds while the resampling seed and the 24 synthetic departure and classical families stay fixed. Across these runs the compact encoder gives family AUROC 0.910, 0.910 and 0.889, versus 0.403, 0.292 and 0.292 for the generic-function control encoder; paired differences are 0.507, 0.618 and 0.597. For the compact encoder, synthetic-classical and cited-test family false-positive rates remain 0/12 and 0/7 in all three runs. However, compact-encoder detection of synthetic departure families is 2/12, 8/12 and 1/12 after recalibrating the threshold in each run. The ranking and specificity increment persists across these seeds; detection at the calibrated threshold varies between runs. With only three seeds, ranges and sample standard deviations describe run-to-run variation; they do not provide confidence intervals.

The provenance assessment in this paragraph concerns a larger point-cloud corpus built from the same formula graph. It is the pool from which the compact encoder's source-verified point clouds were selected (next paragraph) and from which the classical calibration curves of the extrapolation screen were sampled; the large encoder's corpus is described in Supplementary Note~16. The corpus contains 50,898 point clouds associated with 50,898 unique knowledge-graph node identifiers. Matching every identifier to the 5.6-million-node source graph shows that all 50,898 point clouds lack explicit year metadata and all lack source or citation metadata. A conservative keyword screen finds at least 1,686 post-1900 records (3.31\%), including named Eddington, Bondi, Prandtl, Debye, Fermi, Landau, Einstein and Bardeen--Cooper--Schrieffer (BCS) relations. The keyword count is a lower bound; the remaining records have not been fully classified by date. Exact normalized-expression duplicates form 4,381 groups containing 16,258 records; structural-template duplicates form 4,590 groups containing 26,726 records. The corpus is therefore not historically pure, and its 50,898 point clouds are not 50,898 independent laws.

The compact encoder's corpus contains 268 point clouds from 51 cited pre-1900 law families, each dated by a verified source, split without family overlap into 205/17/46 training/validation/test datasets (40/4/7 families). The formula expressions were drawn from a machine-assembled formula graph. The 243 formula records audited for this corpus (covering 49 of its 51 families) comprise 116 generated with language models, 92 combinatorially constructed, 32 mined automatically and 3 manually curated records. A record was admitted only by exact name match to, or derivation ancestry from, a law family in a registry of dated pre-1900 sources, which supplies the date and citation but no formulas; the expressions were not individually checked against the original source texts. The compact encoder is trained on the 205 classical datasets alone, using neither synthetic departure examples nor deformation labels. On the 192-dataset benchmark, the compact encoder's k-nearest-neighbour (kNN) AUROC is 0.854 and shrinkage-Mahalanobis AUROC is 0.895, compared with 0.329 and 0.288 for a matched untrained network. The variant supplied with unit information reaches 0.449/0.432. Because the synthetic benchmark has no physical dimensions, this comparison tests a mismatch in metadata format. In the evaluation grouped by family, dataset AUROC is 0.870 and family AUROC is 0.903 (family-bootstrap 95\% CI [0.729, 1.000]). The calibrated threshold detects 2/12 synthetic departure families, flags 0/12 synthetic classical families and leaves all seven held-out cited classical families below threshold at the family level. One of 46 cited test records is flagged. Detection at the calibrated threshold is low, and the experiment uses a small corpus and synthetic data.

We also compare encoders with and without unit information using the same metadata format. We generate six deformations of every one of the 46 cited test datasets while preserving the source dataset's x/y SI exponent vectors; 34/46 datasets carry at least one nonzero exponent. The evaluation unit is a law family: within each of seven families we take one median for clean data and one for each of saturation, step, kink, splice, roll-off and crossover. Family-balanced AUROC is 0.684 for the encoder supplied with unit information and 0.922 for the compact encoder, a paired difference of $-0.238$ (5,000-replicate family bootstrap 95\% CI [$-0.439$, $-0.082$]). Both leave 7/7 clean families below threshold. At separately calibrated thresholds, however, the two variants detect 0/42 and 4/42 family-by-deformation cells; neither exceeds threshold when scores are pooled to the median for each deformation family. The ranking advantage of the compact encoder persists with matched metadata formats. Detection at the calibrated thresholds remains low. The comparison uses synthetic deformations, one paired training seed and seven test families; the inherited unit vectors were not manually re-derived for each formula.

Two retrospective calibration choices give different representation-screen verdicts on historical measurements. Under a 40-family reference only copper exceeds the threshold (p = 2/41), while a separate 17-dataset validation reference gives FIRAS/copper kNN p = 6/18 and 2/18; neither exceeds its threshold. Mahalanobis gives the same minimum p = 1/18 to FIRAS, copper and the Boyle control, indicating sensitivity to the calibration choice. Michelson's speed-of-light series, a classical control with a near-constant response, is assigned by the rule to the named-prediction test; scored by the representation screen regardless, it receives kNN p = 1/18 and exceeds the validation threshold, a false positive for this classical control. Bertozzi and Onnes have fewer than 20 points and are outside the encoder's operating range (at least 20 points). The deformation-supervised probe, an earlier encoder trained with examples of deformations, has explicit morphology supervision; leave-one-deformation-family-out AUROCs are 0.521 (saturation), 0.636 (step), 0.407 (kink), 0.700 (splice), 0.735 (roll-off) and 0.635 (crossover), mean 0.606. Training without post-1900 physical formulas can still include an anomaly-shape prior through deformation supervision.

\section{Supplementary Note 11: Comparison with successor predictions}

We rescore the same measurements on the same grid and under the same error model using the pre-1900 and successor predictions, with 1,000 parametric-bootstrap replicates for each. All pre-1900 predictions return p = 1/1001. Successor-prediction p-values are 1.000 for FIRAS/Planck, 0.089 for Bertozzi/relativity, 0.001 for copper/Debye-plus-electronic, 1.000 for Onnes/superconducting transition and 0.900 for Millikan/photoelectricity. The discrepancy statistics fall by factors of $4.03\times10^{6}$, $9.58\times10^{5}$, $3.86\times10^{3}$, $7.29\times10^{10}$ and $1.99\times10^{4}$, respectively. Four of five cases meet the paired criterion: the pre-1900 prediction is rejected and the successor prediction is not rejected at 5\%.

The copper failure is sensitive to the assumed relative error floor of the NIST reference polynomial. Both pre-1900 and successor predictions are rejected at error floors of 2\%, 3\% and 5\%; the successor prediction is not rejected at 7.5\% and above, while the pre-1900 prediction remains rejected. Because an externally justified covariance is unavailable, the main analysis retains the prespecified 5\% floor, at which the successor prediction is rejected. The comparison is retrospective, and its error models are assumed, not externally validated.

As a negative control for model flexibility, we select a low-dimensional wrong-shape family for each dataset: affine for FIRAS, quadratic-through-origin for Bertozzi, global power law for copper, smooth quadratic for Onnes and inverse-frequency for Millikan. All five remain rejected after BH correction (four p = 0.000999; Millikan p = 0.00115 from 871 valid replicates; all $q \leq 0.0015$).

We also test wrong-mechanism forms under the same grids and error models. The five specified predictions are
\[
I_F(x)=\frac{A x^3}{\exp(bx)+1},\qquad
\beta^2=1-\left(1+\frac{K}{m_pc^2}\right)^{-2},
\]
\[
C(T)=C_\infty\frac{z^2e^z}{(e^z-1)^2}+\gamma T\;(z=\Theta_E/T),\qquad
R(T)=R_0e^{-\Theta_A/T},\qquad
V(\nu)=V_0+\frac{(h\nu)^2}{2m_ec^2e}.
\]
They assign, respectively, a fermionic occupation spectrum to FIRAS, a proton rest-energy scale to the Bertozzi electron beam, an Einstein oscillator to copper, continuous Arrhenius transport to Onnes and a Compton-recoil scale to Millikan. Amplitude and temperature scale are fitted for FIRAS; the proton mass is fixed; plateau, Einstein temperature and electronic coefficient are fitted for copper; resistance scale and activation temperature are fitted for Onnes; and only the voltage offset is fitted for Millikan. Each exponent or ratio is dimensionless and every prediction has the observed output dimension. All five wrong-mechanism models remain rejected after 1,000 valid parametric-bootstrap replicates per case (p = q = 1/1001 throughout). The wrong-shape and wrong-mechanism alternatives were chosen after the datasets were known.

\section{Supplementary Note 12: Simulated instrument effects}

Classical synthetic relations were corrupted without changing their physical law. At maximum tested severity, false-positive rates at the nominal 5\% threshold are 0 for a 20\% end-to-end calibration drift, 0.40 for sensor clipping at the 60th response percentile and 0.633 for censoring up to the 45th percentile. The rates are 0.092 for x-axis noise of 8\% of the range, 0.108 for six-standard-deviation outliers affecting 8\% of points, 0.025 for first-order autoregressive [AR(1)] noise with coefficient 0.95 and 0 for nine-fold heteroscedastic noise growth. Within these simulations, saturation and censoring have the largest false-positive rates. The rates apply to the tested generators.

\section{Supplementary Note 13: Boundary intervals}

An ordinary 90\% measurement-bootstrap interval covers the simulated onset only 53.9\% of the time overall and 8.5\% for steps. We therefore calibrate the normalized absolute localization error on 899 independent simulations, separately by sample size. The 90th-percentile radius is 0.219, 0.169 and 0.120 of the $x$ range for $n = 40$, 80 and 160. Applied unchanged to 900 held-out simulations, overall coverage is 0.913 (Wilson 95\% CI [0.893, 0.930]). By family, coverage is 0.939 for steps, 0.894 for saturation, 0.944 for kinks, 0.878 for smooth turnovers and 0.911 for crossovers. Coverage is marginal over the specified generators.

\section{Supplementary Note 14: Candidate measurement datasets}

Forty-four files were downloaded from the NIST Dataplot/Statistical Reference Dataset index; source URLs were recorded. All 44 contain structurally extractable numeric blocks, and no two files are identical. \section{Supplementary Note 16: Large encoder at the pre-1900 horizon and horizon extension}

The large-encoder analysis was specified after the retrospective analyses above but before its temporal results were known. The specification fixed the model, code and configuration, the 30-epoch model-selection rule, four score components, equal weights, aggregation, family/group-level AUROC, grouped bootstrap and a conditional horizon extension.  The later-formula lists and dates were known when this specification was written; temporal scores and label-dependent model choices were unavailable. After the results were known, we restricted the learned comparison to the source-verified pre-1900 training horizon; the completed pre-1950 extension is reported separately below.

The large encoder has 20,908,547 parameters. Its point-cloud encoder is a six-block permutation-invariant Set Transformer with width 384, eight attention heads, four learned pooling queries and a 256-dimensional normalized output. A training-only expression-tree network represents symbolic formulas as abstract syntax trees (ASTs) and uses four edge-aware graph updates followed by two graph-local transformer layers. One of the four local update multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) is replaced by a radial-basis-function Kolmogorov--Arnold-network bottleneck with 96 channels and eight fixed radial basis locations; the remaining graph network is unchanged. Multi-positive contrastive alignment uses families where available, relation groups for pre-1950 candidates and exact formulas where pre-1900 identities are unresolved. Training also encourages the same formula's resampled copies to have similar embeddings. The query-time model sees only an unordered point cloud normalized by median and interquartile range. It receives no graph, symbolic expression, units, source, date or anomaly/deformation label.

The point-cloud corpus contains 1,874,880 clouds from 19,605 solvable formula reductions, together with 19,605 AST graphs containing 275,722 nodes and 512,234 directed edges. The large encoder is trained on 880,896 pre-1900 clouds derived from 3,441 formula reductions and 91 sets treated as the same law during training, spanning 64 law families; the model trains for 30 epochs at batch size 1,024. These resampled copies are not independent laws. Its 11,648-cloud calibration set contains 91 formulas, and its 62,336-cloud test set contains 487 formulas whose scores are summarized over nine control groups. The same-generator set contains 96,064 clouds from 1,501 formulas. The separate benchmark sourced from historical citations contains 304 datasets from 38 families dated 1901--1950 and 136 datasets from 17 post-1950 families, with eight resampled copies per family that vary nuisance parameters and noise. The 848,697-row pre-1900 and 726,702-row nominal pre-1950 symbolic augmentation files are intended for a separate symbolic objective and are excluded from the large encoder's training examples.

At the pre-1900 horizon, three reference sets are constructed only from the large encoder's training data: expression-tree embeddings of the training formulas, centroids of formulas treated as the same law and point-cloud prototypes (mean point-cloud embeddings for each formula). The four distance components are the nearest formula embedding, the law centroid and the point-cloud prototype (top-eight cosine distance to each of these reference sets) and the Mahalanobis distance (a deterministic shrinkage-Mahalanobis distance to the distribution of the point-cloud prototypes). Each score is transformed by its empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) on pre-1900 calibration formulas, and the four ranks are averaged with equal weights. We take median scores over clouds within formulas, then over formulas within law families or relation groups. After the large-encoder results were known, a separate exploratory horizon extension was trained on 959,744 clouds: the same 880,896 pre-1900 clouds plus 78,848 pre-1950 clouds generated by the same process. The added formulas lack a complete source-by-source date list, so this model is excluded from the learned comparison at the original horizon.

The large encoder compares held-out pre-1900 groups with the 38 later families dated 1901--1950, the 17 later families and their 55-family union. An untrained network with the same architecture and initialization seed is scored by the same evaluator as a descriptive random-feature control. This control tests whether temporal separation appears without training. The reported quantity is continuous ranking of later families. The extension compares its training-horizon groups with the 17 later families and scores the same 38 families under both models. 

The regenerated-control analysis was chosen after the large-encoder results were known to examine possible differences between the benchmark generators. Its construction was specified before the controls were scored. All nine pre-1900 control groups are regenerated using one representative formula reduction per group and the later-family benchmark's point count, x-jitter, repeat count, noise range and heteroscedastic profile. The same trained model, reference embeddings, calibration CDF and aggregation compare later families with these regenerated controls; the untrained network is scored identically.

\section{Supplementary Note 18: Screens on measured series}

\subsection{Design}

Sixteen series are taken unmodified from the NIST Dataplot and Statistical Reference Dataset collections and are referred to by their NIST dataset identifiers (for example HAHN1), listed with their relations in Supplementary Table~\ref{stab:measured}. For each series, \nolinkurl{real_measurement_case_registry.json} specifies the numeric block, the abscissa and response columns, the 1899 prediction, the basis for placing the phenomenon inside or outside the pre-1900 model set, the mechanism group and the primary source. The horizon class of a series follows from the measured phenomenon and its source, and is fixed before any score is computed. Series are sorted by abscissa and thinned uniformly to at most 2,000 points.

A mechanism group, the unit of inference, comprises the measured series produced by the same physical mechanism; a subset or decimation of a series belongs to the group of the full series. The three superconducting magnetization files (BENNETT5, BENNETT6 and BENNETT7), recorded in one 1994 campaign, form a single group, so the sixteen series give fourteen mechanism groups, five outside the pre-1900 model set and nine classical or metrological.

Where a file header and its stored numeric block disagree on column order, the order is resolved against certified values. In HAHN1 the block stores temperature before the expansion coefficient, so the first row reads 24.41 K and 0.591. ROSZMAN1 carries no header: its two columns are the principal quantum number and the term energy of a sulfur series, and the certified quantum defect is absent. The stored curve follows the inverse-square relation in principal quantum number given by Rydberg in 1888, placing it inside the 1899 knowledge horizon.

\subsection{Scoring}

Both screens retain their trained models and calibration. The representation screen uses the compact encoder and reference embeddings reconstructed deterministically from its pre-1900 training split. It applies the near-constant assignment rule and only rescales the resampled copies of measured series; no synthetic noise is added. No series meets the near-constant criterion, so all sixteen are scored by the representation screen; the standard-resistor series is closest, with relative response variation 0.0023 against the specified cutoff 0.001.

The extrapolation screen repeats direction choice, window search, model selection, parameter fitting and scoring on eight subsamples per series. The subsample size is the largest supported calibration size, selected from $n = 5$, 9, 43 and 120. Eight series support $n = 120$, one supports $n = 43$ and seven support $n = 9$. We take median scores within each series and then within each mechanism group. Bootstrap intervals for the grouped AUROC use 2,000 replicates resampled over mechanism groups.

We report raw screen scores. All 128 scores from resampled copies are available in \nolinkurl{real_measurement_case_view_scores.csv}, and the per-series and per-group medians in \nolinkurl{real_measurement_case_evaluation.json}.

Copper thermal expansion is the highest-ranked post-1900 group under the learned score. For example, the reported coefficient is 0.591 at 24~K and 21.09 at 852~K. Classical equipartition of lattice modes gives a temperature-independent coefficient; the observed fall towards zero below the Debye temperature is the departure. The cold Bose-gas density profile is the highest-ranked group under the extrapolation score, at 13.980 against a median of 0.566 across the classical and metrological groups.

Circular-interference transmittance is the shape control. Its transmittance rises by three and a half orders of magnitude to a sharp resonance at 451 nm and falls again within 20 nm. Among the sixteen series, this sharp shape provides a control accounted for by classical wave optics. Its learned score is 0.0630 and its extrapolation score 1.946, above the classical median of 0.566; the series is calibrated at $n = 9$, so the $n = 120$ reference threshold of 3.39 shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{sfig:measured}a does not apply to it. This classically explained resonance has a low learned score despite its sharp shape.

Superconducting magnetization relaxation is the converse. Superconducting relaxation has no pre-1900 account, but $M(t)$ against log time is close to a straight line over the sampled decade. The three files have the lowest, second-lowest and fourth-lowest extrapolation scores in the collection. The Pound--Rebka and Millikan relations also retain generic shapes despite discrepancies from named pre-1900 predictions. The named-prediction test examines these discrepancies.

The two highest learned scores over the whole collection belong to a five-year standard-resistor record and a 23-point polymer-impedance curve. A Rydberg series, inside the horizon since 1888, ranks above every post-1900 group on the learned score (0.1588 against a highest post-1900 group value of 0.1586). On measured data, this pattern is consistent with the observation process setting the largest learned-score differences. Whether saturation and censoring, tested for the extrapolation screen in Supplementary Note 12, account for these learned-score differences has not been tested.

\subsection{Scope}

The bootstrap intervals are based on fourteen mechanism groups. The nine negative groups are weighted towards instrument transfer curves, and the seven series that support only the $n = 9$ calibration size are scored on eight subsamples of nine points drawn from series of 21 to 40 points. The scores from resampled copies allow the ordering to be recomputed with different groups, a different aggregation, or a restriction to the eight series that support $n = 120$.
