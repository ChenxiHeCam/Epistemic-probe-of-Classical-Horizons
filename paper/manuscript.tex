\begin{abstract}
Physical predictions can fail before a theory explaining the failure is available. We ask how to test such a failure while restricting the knowledge used to construct the prediction. We make the historically admissible model class an explicit input to a calibrated test: a calendar date, the knowledge horizon, fixes which physical laws may construct the prediction, and later theories enter only for retrospective comparison. Measurements are compared with a named prediction under an observation model, and generic screens rank relations by how unlike the admissible laws they are. At an 1899 horizon, all five pre-1900 predictions are rejected on five selected historical datasets. On the same measurement grids and error models, four of the five historical successors are not rejected, whereas retrospectively chosen alternatives with the wrong shape or mechanism remain rejected. Millikan's nearly perfect photoelectric line rejects the classical prediction: curve familiarity is not physical compatibility. A pre-1900 representation ranks later law families above pre-1900 controls, with performance that depends on how the reference formulas were collected and generated. The historical constraint thus allows the predictive domain of specified physical models to be tested without using a successor theory to construct the test.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The failure of a physical prediction can be evident before its explanation is known. Classical predictions for radiation, heat capacity and electrical resistance provide familiar examples: measurements outside a successful regime eventually required quantum descriptions. A test of predictive failure, however, requires a reference prediction and an account of the measurement errors. The question is how to construct that reference while excluding the knowledge that later explained the departure.

Existing methods quantify discrepancy, detect change or search for replacement laws. Bayesian model discrepancy compares observations with a simulator~\cite{kennedy2001bayesian}, conformal prediction calibrates residuals~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}, change-point methods locate distributional shifts~\cite{page1954cusum} and out-of-distribution detectors score departures from a reference distribution~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. Machine-learning methods interpolate, forecast or extrapolate scientific data~\cite{wang2023discovery,xu2021extrapolate}, and symbolic regression searches for governing expressions~\cite{guimera2020bayesian,udrescu2020feynman}. Each takes the reference model or the space of candidate laws as given. None specifies which physical models were admissible before the later explanation existed.

We make that specification an explicit input. A calendar date, the knowledge horizon, fixes the knowledge a test may use: a relation is admissible if its physical content follows from laws and principles documented before the horizon. The pre-horizon prediction for a measured quantity is built from admissible relations only. The historical successor that later explained a departure enters afterwards, for comparison on the same measurements, so it never defines the discrepancy being tested.

EPOCH (Epistemic Probe of Classical Horizons) separates generic screens from tests of named predictions. A representation screen measures how far a curve lies from pre-horizon relations in the embedding of a neural encoder trained only on those relations. A simple-form extrapolation screen, which fits power, exponential, linear or constant forms, fits the best form over the stretch of data it describes, the fitted regime, and scores its extrapolation over the remainder, the extrapolation region. Both screens identify unfamiliar curves; neither refers to a physical theory. A named-prediction test compares the residuals of the measurements from a specified pre-horizon prediction with a null distribution simulated under the stated measurement errors. It returns one of four verdicts: compatibility over the measured range; localized incompatibility, when the prediction fits a regime and fails beyond an estimated boundary; whole-range incompatibility, when the prediction is rejected over the sampled range and no compatible regime is identified; or abstention, when the inputs do not support a test.

We use an 1899 horizon, for which classical relations can be reconstructed from dated sources. We first show how the horizon defines the admissible prediction for a dataset. We then test five historical predictions on measurements and replace each by its successor on the same grid and error model. Next we ask how far the generic screens rank later laws above pre-1900 laws and where they stop being informative. Finally we show that the observation model determines both rejection and the localization of a boundary.

\section{Results}

\subsection{A historical cutoff defines the admissible prediction}

Figure~\ref{fig:concept}a places dated law families on either side of the 1899 horizon. Relations sharing a law form a law family, and training and evaluation are divided by family. Pre-1900 families supply both the relations on which the representation screen is trained and the predictions a test may use; later families enter only for evaluation. A test (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}b) compares measurements with the admissible pre-1900 prediction under an observation model, the stated description of the errors, censoring and grid, and returns a verdict. The generic screens form a side branch that flags relations for testing (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}b,c). They receive only a point cloud, the normalized $(x, y)$ pairs of a relation without formula, units or labels. Where a fitted regime is sampled, the extrapolation screen can also localize a departure, which gives a descriptive classification; rejection of a named physical prediction comes from the named-prediction test. A fixed rule assigns each dataset to the tests its inputs support (Methods; Supplementary Fig.~1). Figure~\ref{fig:concept}d shows the verdicts on real data, and Table~\ref{tab:evidence} summarizes the evidence.

\subsection{Historical predictions fail where their successors fit}

All five pre-1900 predictions are rejected, and on the same grids and error models four of the five successor predictions are not rejected (Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}). We selected these measurements as known classical failures. The COBE-FIRAS spectrum and Bertozzi's electron data lack a measured classical regime, whereas five heat-capacity curves and mercury resistance contain one, so the cases distinguish failure across the sampled range from failure beyond a fitted regime (Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}a--d). Millikan's photoelectric line tests a different distinction: a familiar mathematical shape can violate a named physical prediction.

The COBE-FIRAS monopole spectrum of the cosmic microwave background~\cite{fixsen1996firas} has 43 points and, at $T = 2.725$~K, samples $x = h\nu/kT$ from 1.2 to 11.3. The complete range lies outside $x \ll 1$, where the Rayleigh--Jeans law~\cite{rayleigh1900,jeans1905} holds. Its prediction, intensity proportional to $\nu^{2}$, over-predicts the intensity above the peak by orders of magnitude, by an amount that depends on the fitting window (Supplementary Note~5). The verdict is whole-range incompatibility; the data do not locate a transition from a classical regime.

Bertozzi's electron measurements~\cite{bertozzi1964} likewise lie outside the non-relativistic regime. For kinetic energy against $\beta^{2} = v^{2}/c^{2}$, the classical law $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$ crosses $\beta^{2} = 1$ at 0.26~MeV and reaches $\beta^{2} = 58.7$ at 15~MeV, equivalent to $7.7c$. The lowest measured point is already $0.87c$, and the measured $\beta^{2}$ values approach 1. The verdict is whole-range incompatibility with the Newtonian energy--velocity prediction.

The Dulong--Petit law~\cite{dulong1819} predicts temperature-independent molar heat capacity. Five of the six NIST cryogenic reference curves contain a high-temperature plateau, the fitted regime, and depart from that constant at low temperature, giving a candidate localized incompatibility; beryllium lacks a plateau and gives whole-range incompatibility. For oxygen-free high-conductivity copper, the extrapolated plateau over-predicts the 4~K reference value by $3.7\times10^{3}$. The six reference curves share a low-temperature mechanism and are grouped together.

Kamerlingh Onnes's mercury resistance values~\cite{onnes1911} contain a smooth normal-state trend followed by a discontinuous drop. The transition is placed at 4.20~K, a temperature fixed from the historical record in the paired prediction test. The smooth normal-state and residual-resistance models~\cite{drude1900} are tested with figure-reading uncertainty and the reported resistance bound below $10^{-5}\,\Omega$ in the observation model.

Millikan's 1916 measurement~\cite{millikan1916} demonstrates that curve familiarity is not physical compatibility. Stopping potential is linear in frequency, $V_{\mathrm{stop}} = (h/e)(\nu - \nu_{0})$, with $R^{2} = 0.99995$ and a slope recovering $h/e$ to 0.3\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}e). The extrapolation screen accepts the shape, yet the named-prediction test on the same four measurements rejects the classical prediction of frequency-independent photoelectron energy. The fitted intercept gives $\nu_{0} = 4.39\times10^{14}$~Hz; all four frequencies exceed $\nu_{0}$, so the verdict is whole-range incompatibility.

Each pre-1900 prediction was then replaced by its historical successor and scored on identical measurements and grids under the same error model (Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}f); successor nuisance parameters are fitted to the same data where specified, and the Onnes successor is a phenomenological transition form. All pre-1900 predictions are rejected (bootstrap $p = 0.001$). The successors are not rejected for FIRAS under Planck's law~\cite{planck1901}, Bertozzi under relativistic kinetic energy~\cite{einstein1905relativity}, Onnes under a superconducting transition model and Millikan under the photoelectric relation~\cite{einstein1905photoelectric}; individual $p$-values are given in Fig.~\ref{fig:cases} and Supplementary Note~11. The copper successor, a Debye-plus-electronic form~\cite{debye1912}, is rejected at the primary 5\% reference-curve error floor ($p = 0.001$) and passes when the floor is raised to 7.5\%; the floor stands in for a covariance model of the reference curve, which is unavailable.

Wrong-shape and wrong-mechanism alternatives, one of each per case and retrospectively chosen after the datasets were known, remain rejected: all five wrong-shape alternatives after Benjamini--Hochberg correction ($q \leq 0.0015$) and all five dimensionally coherent wrong-mechanism alternatives (1,000 bootstrap replicates; $p = q = 1/1001$). The tested alternative forms therefore do not reproduce the successors' agreement (Supplementary Note~11).

Measured classical controls retain their expected forms: Solar-System orbits~\cite{kepler1619} and the four Galilean moons recover Kepler's exponent of 3/2 to four figures (1.500 and 1.500), and Boyle's 1662 pressure--volume table~\cite{boyle1662} gives $-0.998$; all three fall below the extrapolation screen's 0.5 screening threshold (Supplementary Fig.~2a--c). The extrapolation screen does not reject FIRAS, copper heat capacity, Bertozzi or Onnes after Benjamini--Hochberg correction (Supplementary Note~4), so the rejections come from the named-prediction tests, and the localized verdicts for heat capacity and resistance are descriptive classifications.

\subsection{Generic screens rank later laws but do not replace a test of a physical prediction}

A pre-1900 representation ranks later laws, with performance that depends on how the reference formulas were collected and generated. The large encoder, trained only on point clouds from dimensionless reductions of pre-1900 laws, ranks 55 later law families above nine held-out pre-1900 control groups with AUROC 0.925 [0.851, 0.982] (Fig.~\ref{fig:screening}a). AUROC, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, is the probability that a randomly chosen later family outranks a pre-1900 group, counting ties as one half; 0.5 is chance. Brackets give 95\% bootstrap intervals over families or groups. The untrained network, the same network with its initial random weights, gives 0.649 on the same comparison, and each of the encoder's four distance components improves on it (Supplementary Fig.~4d).

The ranking depends on how the reference formulas were collected and generated. Regenerating the nine control groups with the later families' sampling and noise still gives AUROC 0.857 [0.741, 0.945], against 0.545 for the untrained network (Fig.~\ref{fig:screening}a); these regenerated controls were added after the primary results were known. The dependence appears on the same-generator set, a separate collection of later formulas dated 1901--1950 and produced by the generator of the training corpus, whose family composition also differs from the 55-family benchmark: there the trained encoder gives 0.536 [0.424, 0.643], near chance. The learned ranking therefore reflects which formulas were collected and how they were generated, as well as when the laws were found. An exploratory extension to a 1950 horizon, whose added formulas lack complete source-by-source dates, gives 0.453 [0.228, 0.678] on post-1950 families (Supplementary Note~16).

Simple-form extrapolation also ranks the later families above pre-1900 controls, without a trained network. Against eleven cited pre-1900 families excluded from encoder training, the extrapolation screen gives AUROC 0.888 [0.800, 0.959], compared with 0.669 [0.534, 0.798] for a compact encoder trained on 205 point clouds from 40 source-verified pre-1900 families (Fig.~\ref{fig:screening}b). The compact encoder ranks 12 synthetic departure families, curves built to leave a classical form, above 12 classical families with family AUROC 0.903, against 0.375 for an encoder matched in architecture, training and data size but trained on generic smooth expressions (Supplementary Fig.~4c; Supplementary Note~8). Ranking is stronger than calibrated detection: at a threshold targeted to 5\% false positives, the extrapolation screen detects synthetic departures with true-positive rate 0.271 [0.073, 0.500] (Supplementary Note~4), and at their calibrated thresholds each screen flags 7 of the 55 later families and none of the 11 controls (Supplementary Note~15).

The families the screens miss show why a ranking does not test a physical prediction. Threshold and piecewise relations separate more strongly by extrapolation, whereas affine, power and exponential relations can remain close to the admitted forms. Later laws with familiar shapes, such as special relativity, the photoelectric threshold and radioactive decay, are flagged by neither screen and require a named-prediction test (Supplementary Note~15). Eleven further generated cases, assembled after the primary scores were known, illustrate the same distinction (Supplementary Fig.~5; Supplementary Note~17). On sixteen measured NIST series, grouped into five post-1900 and nine classical or metrological mechanism groups and collected during method development, the compact encoder gives AUROC 0.62 [0.29, 0.89] and the extrapolation screen 0.78 [0.38, 1.00]; both intervals span chance (Supplementary Fig.~6; Supplementary Note~18).

\subsection{Observation models determine rejection and localization}

The observation process can produce departures even when the underlying classical relation is unchanged. At the highest simulated severity, false-positive rates of the extrapolation screen are 40\% for saturation and 63\% for censoring under calibration targeted to 5\%; the other tested effects remain at or below 11\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:observation}a). A plateau or threshold therefore requires an observation model that accounts for these effects, as the dependence of the copper comparison on its error floor also shows.

Localizing a boundary requires intervals calibrated beyond ordinary measurement resampling. When a compatible regime and its departure are both sampled, a nominal 90\% measurement-bootstrap interval covers 53.9\% of simulated boundaries (358 simulations), and 8.5\% for steps. We calibrated the interval radii on 899 independent simulations, separately by sample size, raising held-out coverage to 91.3\% [89.3\%, 93.0\%] across 900 simulations (Wilson interval; Fig.~\ref{fig:observation}b). This is marginal coverage under the specified generators (Supplementary Note~13).

\section{Discussion}

Detecting an unusual curve is not the same as testing a physical prediction. A screen ranks a curve against a reference corpus; only a named prediction, stated with its observation model, can be rejected. A historical knowledge constraint makes that test possible for predictions whose failure was later explained, without letting the successor define the discrepancy: the reference is built from pre-horizon physics, and the successor enters afterwards on the same grid and error model. In four of the five historical comparisons this replacement turns rejection into non-rejection, whereas the tested alternative forms keep their discrepancies.

Millikan's line and a classical interference resonance show why the distinction matters. Millikan's line has an ordinary shape, yet its frequency dependence rejects the classical prediction (Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}e). Conversely, a sharp interference resonance in a measured transmittance series follows from classical wave optics (Supplementary Fig.~7). Extrapolation of a fitted generic form tests a shape, and the representation screen measures familiarity; a claim about a theory needs that theory's prediction.

EPOCH combines neural representation methods~\cite{wang2023discovery,xu2021extrapolate}, calibrated residuals and model-discrepancy testing~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal,page1954cusum,kennedy2001bayesian}. It is related to model-independent searches in particle physics~\cite{dagnolo2019newphysics} and to machine rediscovery of laws~\cite{guimera2020bayesian,udrescu2020feynman,schmidt2009distilling}. Symbolic regression can seek candidate replacements under a parsimony prior~\cite{he2026searchfree}; testing a restricted prediction and searching for its replacement address different questions. The historical constraint specifies which knowledge can enter the reference prediction.

The evidence has limits. Because the measurements were selected as known failures, the historical comparisons demonstrate the construction but do not give a prospective detection rate. Rejection and localization depend on the observation model, as copper's error-floor sensitivity and the saturation and censoring simulations show. The learned ranking depends on the formula corpus and its generator, and transfer of the screens to measured series and to a later horizon has not been demonstrated. On measured data the largest learned scores belong to a standard-resistor record and a polymer-impedance curve, so a high learned score marks an unusual relation, not a physical mechanism.

A prospective study would pair independently selected measurements with a dated corpus, specified pre-horizon predictions and externally supported error models. With a simulator-based reference, the same specification extends to physical descriptions that supply predictions without a closed-form law, and screening can direct attention to relations that a named-prediction test then examines. Making the admissible knowledge explicit allows the predictive domain of a historical model class to be tested without the theory that replaced it.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Historical admissibility}

A relation is admissible at the knowledge horizon, here 1899, if its physical content follows from laws and principles documented on or before that date. The date is set by the source law, not by when a particular formula text was printed. Mathematical rewriting that adds no physical content, such as nondimensionalization, reduction to one variable or algebraic rearrangement, is allowed. The Rayleigh--Jeans form follows from the equipartition theorem applied to the electromagnetic modes of a cavity, and the normal-state resistance model from Matthiessen's 1864 decomposition of resistance into a temperature-dependent part and a constant residual part~\cite{matthiessen1864}; both are therefore admissible pre-1900 predictions. Where the standard statement of a prediction was published later, as for the Rayleigh--Jeans form in 1900 and 1905 and the Drude model in 1900, we cite that statement as the reference and do not use it as a training source. Only admissible relations enter the training corpora and the predictions a test may fit.

Relations derived from one physical law traced to one dated source form a law family, and measured series produced by one physical mechanism form a mechanism group. Both are the units over which AUROCs are computed and bootstrap intervals resampled; the nine pre-1900 control groups are sets of law families that share one mechanism.

\subsection{Specifying a test}

An EPOCH test specifies
$\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{M},\Theta,P_\varepsilon,I,A,S,\alpha)$. Here
$\mathcal{M}$ lists the admissible models, $\Theta$ the fitted parameters,
$P_\varepsilon$ the observation model, $I$ the fitted regime, $A$ every adaptive choice, $S$ the discrepancy
statistic and $\alpha$ the error rate. The observation model comprises the error distribution, censoring or saturation, and the grid; where no published covariance exists, an error floor assigns a minimum relative error. Direction choice, window search, model selection and parameter fitting all belong to $A$ and are rerun inside every calibration and null replicate.

The named-prediction test computes the residuals of the measurements from the pre-horizon prediction, scaled by the stated errors, and compares their size with a null distribution: the distribution of the statistic over datasets simulated from that prediction on the same $x$-grid with the same error model. Historical named-prediction $p$-values use parametric bootstrap simulations under the specified observation model, with nuisance parameters fitted to the data and refitted in each replicate; their calibration depends on that model and on the bootstrap approximation. Calibrated screening tests use the upper-tail
probability $p=(1+\#\{S_i\geq S_*\})/(n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1)$ with ties counted
conservatively, and dataset scans report Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted
$q$-values~\cite{benjamini1995fdr}. For exchangeable calibration and test units, this rank-based construction has finite-sample validity. AUROC intervals resample law families or mechanism groups. In the synthetic calibrated-detection evaluation of the extrapolation screen, the primary reported quantity is the true-positive rate at a threshold targeted to 5\% false positives on independent calibration,
with the 1\% threshold secondary; screening comparisons report AUROC. The 0.5 threshold on the extrapolation-screen percentile (the calibration median; Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}) selects cases for further testing.

The assignment rule (Supplementary Fig.~1) sends a dataset to the extrapolation screen, whose classification is descriptive, when an admitted form fits a contiguous fitted regime, otherwise to the named-prediction test when a pre-horizon prediction and error model are supplied, and otherwise to abstention; when a fitted regime exists and a pre-horizon prediction and error model are also supplied, both tests are run and reported, as for Millikan's data. Nearly constant responses go to the named-prediction test, which tests a constant against noise, and point clouds with at least 20 observations also receive a learned score.

Specification timing differed across analyses. The historical comparisons are retrospective; the large-encoder temporal analysis was specified before its temporal scores were known, and later scope decisions and added comparisons are identified where they are reported (Supplementary Notes~16 and 17).

\subsection{Extrapolation screen}

The extrapolation screen draws on a fixed vocabulary of power, exponential,
linear and constant forms. On validation data this vocabulary extrapolates better than an eight-form one that adds quadratic, cubic, square-root and logarithmic terms. Given a point cloud, a fixed rule takes
the candidate fitted regime to be the contiguous stretch in which one admitted form
fits with high $R^{2}$, and the extrapolation region to be the complementary
region. The search runs from both ends and fit quality decides whether the low-
or the high-value direction is retained. The screen reports two statistics. The split-conformal
nonconformity~\cite{vovk2005} is the mean absolute residual in the extrapolation region divided by
the 90th percentile of the absolute residuals in the fitted regime, and the
extrapolation-residual ratio is the median absolute log-residual in the extrapolation region divided by its value in the fitted regime. Each
form is fitted from several initializations, and each denominator carries a
floor, one percent of the median response magnitude in the fitted regime for the
nonconformity and 0.01 for the median log-residual, so that a
near-perfect fit cannot produce an unbounded ratio. Relations with fewer than
20 points are calibrated against size-matched simulations.

\subsection{Representation screen}

The representation screen uses a neural encoder trained only on point clouds from pre-horizon relations, with no example of a failure and without any anomaly supervision; its learned score is the distance of a new cloud's embedding from those of the training relations. At query time the encoder receives an unordered, normalized $(x,y)$ cloud and nothing else: no equation, units, date, law name or post-cutoff label.

The large encoder, a permutation-invariant set transformer~\cite{lee2019settransformer} aligned during training to a network that reads the formulas' expression trees, is trained on point clouds derived from 3,441 dimensionless reductions of pre-1900 laws. Held-out calibration and pre-1900 test formulas are separated by law family (Supplementary Fig.~3), and grouping the test formulas by mechanism gives the nine pre-1900 control groups. The weights used for scoring are those selected by a rule fixed before any later-physics evaluation. Its learned score averages four ranks, each transformed by its own pre-1900 calibration empirical cumulative distribution function: cosine distances to the training formulas' expression-tree embeddings, to their means over formulas treated as the same law and to mean point-cloud embeddings for each formula, and a shrinkage-Mahalanobis distance~\cite{mahalanobis1936,ledoit2004} to the distribution of those mean point-cloud embeddings. Scores are summarized by the median over clouds within a formula and then within a law family or control group, and family- and group-level AUROCs use 2,000 grouped-bootstrap replicates; unless stated otherwise, bracketed intervals in this paper are 95\% bootstrap intervals resampled at that level. The untrained network is scored by the identical procedure. Architecture, training schedule and corpus sizes are given in Supplementary Note~16.

To build the regenerated controls we regenerated each of the nine pre-1900 control groups from one representative formula reduction, using the 55-family benchmark's point count, $x$-jitter, repeat count, noise range and heteroscedastic profile, and left the trained weights, calibration, score and aggregation unchanged. The same-generator set and the 1950-horizon extension are described in Supplementary Note~16.

The compact encoder is trained on 205 pre-1900 point clouds from 40 families, without unit information. The formula expressions behind these point clouds were drawn from a machine-assembled formula graph, part of which was generated with language models or by combinatorial construction. A record was admitted only by exact name match to, or derivation ancestry from, a law family in a registry of dated pre-1900 sources, which supplies the family's date and citation; the expressions themselves were not individually checked against the original source texts (Supplementary Note~8). No synthetic departure example enters its training. Leave-family-out calibration holds out each of the 40 training families in full, and the threshold, the 95th percentile of the 40 held-out family scores, is fixed before evaluation. Scores are summarized by the median over eight resampled copies of each dataset and then over the datasets within a family. The generic-function control encoder shares the compact encoder's architecture and training and is trained on 205 point clouds from smooth generic expressions in 40 pseudo-families matched to the historical set in dataset count, family-size profile and the elementary function types present. Architectures, training schedules and the retraining seeds are given in Supplementary Note~8.

\subsection{Post-1900 formula benchmark}

The 55-family benchmark contains 55 law families
first valid between 1901 and 1996, each carrying a primary historical citation; thermionic
emission~\cite{richardson1901} is one example. Every law is represented by a one-dimensional form $y = f(x)$ in dimensionless variables over a fixed
support, sampled eight times at 120 points with varying nuisance parameters,
sampling and 0.5--2.5\% noise, giving 440 datasets across 55 families. Special
reductions, such as the Bardeen--Cooper--Schrieffer interpolation~\cite{bardeen1957}, the two-flavour
neutrino survival curve~\cite{maki1962} and the ideal quantum-Hall
staircase~\cite{klitzing1980}, are listed in Supplementary Note~15. No benchmark family overlaps a family of the pre-1900 corpus.

The compact encoder's learned score is summarized by the median over resampled copies, datasets and families and compared with its fixed threshold. The extrapolation score reruns the full form selection and
extrapolation procedure against the $n = 120$ thresholds of the size-matched classical calibration (785 simulations per size; below). Eleven cited
pre-1900 families excluded from encoder training are the controls, AUROC intervals resample families, and
twelve of the post-1900 laws deliberately overlap the affine, power or exponential forms. The eleven additional generated cases are described in Supplementary Note~17.

\subsection{Historical measurements and controls}

We analyse the published COBE-FIRAS monopole spectrum with its pointwise errors,
five Bertozzi values digitized from the 1964 report, NIST specific-heat reference
polynomials for six materials, Onnes resistance values including upper limits,
Millikan sodium stopping potentials and Solar-System orbital constants. For each case we record the model set, fitted parameters, observation model, fitted regime, adaptive choices, decision rule and output. We evaluate null distributions under the pre-1900 and successor predictions
on the same $x$-grid; explicit error floors stand in for
unavailable covariance and for digitization uncertainty, and we vary them in
sensitivity analysis.

To test whether the reduction in discrepancy is specific to the successor prediction,
five wrong-shape forms, one per case, and five dimensionally coherent wrong-mechanism forms are refitted on the same case grids and observation models with 1,000 parametric-bootstrap
replicates. The wrong-mechanism forms substitute fermionic occupation for photon
statistics, a proton for the electron rest-energy scale, an Einstein oscillator~\cite{einstein1907solid}
for Debye acoustic modes~\cite{debye1912}, Arrhenius transport for the superconducting transition,
and Compton recoil for the photoelectric slope.

Sixteen measured series were taken unmodified from the NIST Dataplot and Statistical Reference Dataset collections; their selection, the specification of each series before scoring, and the scoring and grouping procedure are described in Supplementary Notes~14 and 18.

\subsection{Synthetic suites, baselines and localization}

The baseline comparison uses 36 synthetic datasets, on which the extrapolation screen is compared with its two statistics. Cumulative-sum (CUSUM) change-point detection~\cite{page1954cusum}
and Ramsey's regression specification error test (RESET)~\cite{ramsey1969} are compared with the extrapolation-residual ratio on a separate suite of 16 phenomena (Supplementary Note~3; Supplementary Fig.~2d--f). The mechanism-level benchmark contains
12 classical and 12 synthetic departure families with eight datasets each; its 5\% and 1\%
thresholds come from 785 valid size-matched calibration simulations, and
uncertainty is resampled by departure or classical family. The compact encoder is evaluated on 192 datasets over the same 24 families. Instrument tests apply
calibration drift, sensor saturation, censoring, $x$-axis error, outliers,
correlated noise and heteroscedasticity to classical synthetic relations while
leaving the underlying law intact, and boundary intervals are obtained by
split-conformal calibration of the normalized localization error separately at
$n = 40$, 80 and 160.
