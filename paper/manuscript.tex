\begin{abstract}
Scientific models have finite domains, established only once a successor
theory explains the failure. Here we show that the predictive boundary of a
body of physical knowledge can be tested from that knowledge alone. Only
relations recorded before a chosen date, the knowledge horizon, are admitted,
with no later formula and no example of a departure, and we ask whether
relations it cannot account for rank above those it can. At an 1899 horizon, a
neural encoder trained on pre-1900 laws alone, given only a point cloud without
formula, units or labels, ranks 55 twentieth-century law families above nine
held-out pre-1900 groups with area under the ROC curve (AUROC) 0.925, against
0.649 for the untrained network. A second test, fixed in advance, extrapolates one of
four simple forms fitted to part of each curve and reaches 0.888 against 11
held-out pre-1900 families without a learned representation. When a named
pre-1900 law, the incumbent, is tested against five historical measurements,
the discrepancy disappears under the successful successor prediction in four cases and under none of ten wrong-shape or wrong-mechanism alternatives. The
same construction can be applied to any period with a dated corpus, an admitted
prediction, an observation model and a calibration set.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Physics advances by retiring descriptions that have stopped working. The
Rayleigh--Jeans law, the Dulong--Petit law and the classical account of
electrical resistance were each accurate across the range in which they were
built and each failed outside it, and in each case the failure was explained
only by a successor theory. The domain over which a model can be trusted is
therefore something the field has established in retrospect.

Machine-learning systems for scientific data are built for other targets. They
are optimized to interpolate within a sampled range, to predict forward, or to
recover a compact governing expression from
observations~\cite{wang2023discovery,xu2021extrapolate}. Conformal prediction
calibrates residuals~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}, change-point methods
locate distributional shifts~\cite{page1954cusum}, and Bayesian model
discrepancy compares observations with a simulator~\cite{kennedy2001bayesian}.
Symbolic regression searches for a replacement expression once a discrepancy has
already been found~\cite{guimera2020bayesian,udrescu2020feynman}.

However, none of these operations takes a dated body of physical knowledge as
the object under test. A residual is large relative to some fitted model, a change point is
a shift in some distribution, and a recovered expression is a fit to some data;
in each case the knowledge under test is implicit in the setup. Nor does an
unusual curve shape identify the departure, because a departure from a named
law may keep an ordinary functional form while a familiar mechanism may produce
an unusual one.

We call the date that bounds a body of knowledge its knowledge horizon: only
relations recorded before that date are admitted, and the pre-horizon law tested
against a given dataset is the incumbent. Here we make the knowledge horizon an
explicit input and test it directly with EPOCH (Epistemic Probe of Classical
Horizons). Every test states in advance the laws it may fit, the error model,
the data it fits, every data-dependent choice and the statistic and error rate
it reports (Methods). It returns one of four verdicts: compatible over the measured range; localized incompatibility, with an estimate of where the incumbent stops fitting; whole-range incompatibility; or abstention.

Each test reports three kinds of evidence separately, never as a single
averaged score. The learned component is a neural encoder trained only on
pre-horizon relations, with no example of a departure, which scores a new curve
by its distance from them. The four-form component fits one of four simple forms
(power, exponential, linear or constant) to the part of a curve where an
admitted pre-horizon form fits well, the interior, and measures how far the
remaining points, the frontier, depart from the extrapolation. The theory-conditioned component
compares the measurements with the incumbent prediction and calibrates the
residuals against a null distribution simulated from that prediction on the same
grid with the same errors.

We set the horizon at 1899, the eve of the replacement of the classical model
set by quantum and relativistic physics and a date at which the classical corpus
can be reconstructed from dated primary sources. Every relation admitted to
training is traced to a dated primary source and assigned to a law family, the
set of relations that share one law; training, validation and test data are
divided by family, and no law appears on both sides. Training uses no later
formula and no synthetic breakdown example, and the training and scoring
protocol was fixed before any score on later physics was computed.

We find that an encoder built this way ranks twentieth-century law families
above pre-1900 families withheld from training, and we find that the four-form
component reaches the same ranking without any trained network. On five
historical datasets we find that the discrepancy with the pre-1900 prediction disappears under the successor prediction in four of the five and under no
wrong-shape or wrong-mechanism alternative. EPOCH therefore tests the
predictive domain of a dated body of knowledge without being told where that domain ends. The same construction can be applied to any period for which a dated corpus, an admitted prediction, an observation model and a calibration set exist.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The object of a boundary test}

EPOCH returns one of four verdicts for a set of measurements and a stated set of laws it may fit: compatible over the measured range; localized incompatibility, with an estimate of where the incumbent stops fitting; whole-range incompatibility, with no identifiable boundary; or abstention, when the evidence is insufficient
(Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}). Which component produces the verdict is decided by a rule fixed in advance. The four-form component is used when the measurements contain a
contiguous stretch, the interior, over which a pre-1900 law fits well. The
theory-conditioned component is used otherwise, when an incumbent prediction and
a model of the measurement errors are available. When neither condition is met
the verdict is abstention.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{figures/Figure_1_concept.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Testing a knowledge horizon.} Top, an admitted
  classical form fitted inside the sampled regime (blue) tracks the measurements
  until they leave it; beyond that point the extrapolation and the data
  separate, and the question is whether the separation can be measured and
  located. Bottom, the test as applied here. A point cloud, a set of $(x, y)$
  pairs with no formula, unit or label, enters at the left. A fixed rule
  identifies the interior, the stretch over which an admitted pre-1900 form
  fits, and fits that form there; the extrapolation is compared with the
  frontier by two residual statistics (Methods); and an encoder trained on
  pre-1900 relations alone supplies an independent distance score. The signpost
  carries the verdict and, where an interior is present, the location at which
  compatibility is lost; a fourth output, abstention, applies when neither
  component can be run.}
  \label{fig:concept}
\end{figure}

We calibrate each component against its own reference distribution: the
four-form and learned components against their scores on pre-1900 data, and the
theory-conditioned component against a null distribution simulated from the
incumbent prediction. The four-form component receives a point cloud, the $(x, y)$ pairs of one relation with formula, units
and labels removed, and nothing else. It fits the best of four generic forms
(power, exponential, linear and constant) on the interior and extrapolates the
fit to the frontier, the remainder of the data. Two statistics measure the
failure of the extrapolation. The split-conformal nonconformity is the mean
absolute frontier residual divided by the 90th percentile of the interior
absolute residuals, that is, a frontier residual scaled by the spread of the
residuals where the fit is trusted. The extrapolation-residual ratio is the
median absolute log-residual at the frontier divided by its interior value. Each
denominator carries a small floor (Methods).

The theory-conditioned component computes the residuals of the measurements
from the incumbent prediction, scaled by the stated measurement errors, and compares their size with the null distribution obtained by simulating data from
that prediction on the same grid with the same errors. The learned component is
a neural encoder trained only on point clouds from relations recorded before the
horizon, with no example of a departure; its score is the distance of a new
point cloud's embedding from the embeddings of the training relations.

\subsection{Recognizing later physics from pre-1900 relations alone}

To ask whether later physics can be ranked above pre-1900 physics from the
shape of a relation alone, we trained a 20.9-million-parameter encoder (the
large encoder) on 880,896 point clouds derived from 3,441 dimensionless relations $y = f(x)$, each a reduction of a pre-1900 law. During training, a second
network that reads each formula's expression tree produces a target embedding,
and the point-cloud encoder, which is invariant to the order of the points, is
trained to match it. At inference the encoder receives a normalized point cloud
and nothing else, with no formula, graph, units, date, source or anomaly label.
Every scoring choice was fixed before any score on later physics was computed
(Methods). We report the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), the probability that
a randomly chosen later family scores above a randomly chosen pre-1900 group,
with ties counted as one half, so that 0.5 is chance and 1 is complete
separation; unless stated otherwise,
brackets give 95\% bootstrap intervals resampled over families or groups
(Methods).

On the 38 families dated 1901--1950, each traced to a primary citation, the
trained encoder ranks later physics above the nine held-out pre-1900 groups,
each a set of law families sharing one mechanism, with AUROC
0.921 [0.833, 0.994] (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}a; Fig.~\ref{fig:learned}a),
whereas the same network with its initial random weights and no training steps
(the untrained network) gives 0.589 (Fig.~\ref{fig:learned}c). The 17 post-1950
families give 0.935 [0.823, 1.000] against 0.784 untrained, and the 55-family
union 0.925 [0.851, 0.982] against 0.649. Each of the four distance components
separates the 55-family union on its own, with AUROCs from 0.800 to 0.925
(Fig.~\ref{fig:learned}d).

The later families were generated by a different sampling process from the
pre-1900 training corpus, so sampling alone could in principle produce the
separation. To test this we regenerated all nine pre-1900 control groups, the
regenerated controls, with the point count, x-jitter, repeat count, noise range
and heteroscedastic profile of the benchmark of 55 later law families described
in the next subsection. AUROC is then 0.885 [0.778, 0.965] for the 38
near-future families and 0.857 [0.741, 0.945] for all 55, against 0.516 and
0.545 for the untrained network (Fig.~\ref{fig:learned}a,b), so within the
55-family benchmark the separation does not come from the difference in
generating process alone. A second control, the same-generator set, generates
the later formulas themselves, 1901--1950 formulas in this test, by the same
process as the pre-1900 corpus (Supplementary Note~16). On that set the trained
network gives 0.536 [0.424, 0.643] against 0.594 [0.436, 0.756] untrained: when
the later formulas are drawn by the same generator as the training corpus, the
trained encoder is near chance.

For the compact encoder, evaluated on a separate 192-dataset benchmark of 12
breakdown and 12 classical families, the gain is specific to the historical
corpus. A breakdown family is a set of synthetic curves built by imposing one
kind of departure, such as saturation, a step or a kink, on a classical form;
such curves are used only for evaluation, never for training or for setting
thresholds. A corpus reconstructed from cited pre-1900 sources supplies 268
point clouds from 51
cited pre-1900 law families, split by family into 205/17/46 training, validation
and test datasets. A compact encoder trained on the 205 classical datasets (the
compact encoder throughout, trained with no unit information) reaches
dataset-level AUROC 0.854 by k-nearest-neighbour distance and 0.895 by shrinkage
Mahalanobis distance, against 0.329 and 0.288 for matched random features, and
family-level AUROC 0.903 [0.729, 1.000].

In contrast, a generic-function control encoder that we trained on generic
smooth expressions, with identical architecture, seed, schedule, dataset count, family-size profile and functional-signature histogram (the elementary function types each formula contains), reaches
0.375 [0.167, 0.618]; the paired difference is 0.528 [0.264, 0.764]. Across
three paired seeds the two ranges are 0.889--0.910 and 0.292--0.403. At the threshold fixed on held-out families the encoder detects 2/12 breakdown families with no false positive among the classical controls, and this count is not stable across the three
seeds (2/12, 8/12 and 1/12; Supplementary Note~8). A deformation control that
retains each dataset's physical units gives family-balanced AUROC 0.684
with dimensional labels and 0.922 without them, so the ranking obtained without unit information is not carried by units.

\subsection{Explicit extrapolation as independent evidence}

We next asked whether explicit extrapolation alone ranks later families above
held-out pre-1900 families, without a learned representation. The 55-family
benchmark contains 55 named
physical-law families first valid from 1901 to 1996, each traced to a primary
citation. Eight noisy 120-point samples of each give
440 formula-generated point clouds (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}b); eleven cited
pre-1900 families excluded from encoder training are the controls. Each law family is scored by the median over its point clouds (Methods). The four-form component ranks later families above the
earlier holdouts with
AUROC 0.888 [0.800, 0.959], and the compact encoder with 0.669 [0.534, 0.798].
An earlier evaluation of the extrapolation test, with its decision rule set before any label was seen, over 200 pre-1900 formula datasets and 30 synthetic datasets of five breakdown families, gives AUROC 0.88, with recall 0.90
and a false-positive rate of 0.24 at the 0.5 screening threshold, the median of
the calibration scores, which selects cases for further testing.

The ranking also varies with functional form. Learned and four-form AUROCs are
0.629/0.795 for 12 later affine, power or exponential laws, 0.600/0.982 for five
threshold or piecewise families, and 0.909/0.864 for six nonmonotone or
oscillatory families, so the two scores respond differently to smooth familiar
forms, to thresholds and to nonmonotone curves.

When we apply both components to 11 further twentieth-century cases
(Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}c--g), the two components diverge again: Casimir
plates~\cite{casimir1948} give 1.000/0.892, Cherenkov emission~\cite{frank1937}
0.823/0.965, cosmic acceleration~\cite{riess1998,perlmutter1999} 0.908/0.429 and
Pound--Rebka redshift~\cite{pound1960} 0.071/0.277. Cases that report a single value or only a few points, including the Lamb shift~\cite{lamb1947} and the electron anomalous magnetic moment~\cite{schwinger1948}, are assigned to the theory-conditioned component or to abstention and are not part of this comparison (Supplementary Note~17). The
calibrated detection rate of the four-form component at a fixed false-positive
rate is reported below (Calibration, thresholds and the observation process).

\subsection{Historical measurements at the 1899 horizon}

We then scored four historical datasets that illustrate two of the four
verdicts, whole-range incompatibility and localized incompatibility
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}a--d); a fifth, Millikan's photoelectric
measurement, is treated separately below. The four-form component's initial
screen gives percentiles (the fraction of size-matched classical calibration
simulations scoring lower) of 0.95 for FIRAS, 0.95 for copper specific heat,
0.66 for Bertozzi and 0.66 for Onnes; whether an interior is present then
determines which of the two outputs applies. Supplementary Note~4 gives finite-sample
tail probabilities and the multiplicity calculation.

The COBE-FIRAS monopole spectrum of the cosmic microwave
background~\cite{fixsen1996firas} has 43 points and, at $T = 2.725$~K, samples
$x = h\nu/kT$ from 1.2 to 11.3, entirely outside the regime $x \ll 1$ in which
the Rayleigh--Jeans law~\cite{rayleigh1900,jeans1905} holds. The incumbent
prediction, intensity proportional to $\nu^{2}$, over-predicts the measured intensity above the peak by 475-fold to 3,000-fold, depending on how many of the lowest frequencies are used to fit the law. Fitting to the lowest 20\% of frequencies gives a bootstrap interval of [1,500, 2,400]. No interior is present, and the verdict is whole-range
incompatibility with the Rayleigh--Jeans prediction.

Bertozzi's electron measurements\cite{bertozzi1964} report kinetic energy against
$\beta^{2} = v^{2}/c^{2}$. The classical law $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$ crosses
$\beta^{2} = 1$ at 0.26~MeV and reaches $\beta^{2} = 58.7$ at 15~MeV,
equivalent to $7.7c$, whereas the measured $\beta^{2}$ values approach 1. The
lowest point is already $0.87c$, placing the complete series outside a
non-relativistic interior, and the output is whole-range incompatibility with
the Newtonian energy--velocity prediction.

The Dulong--Petit law~\cite{dulong1819} predicts temperature-independent molar heat capacity,
whereas the NIST cryogenic reference curves for six solids fall at low
temperature. Five materials contain a high-temperature plateau, the interior,
and support localized incompatibility relative to the constant model; beryllium
gives whole-range incompatibility. Interior $R^{2}$ ranges from 0.965 to 0.998
and extrapolation-residual ratios from 127 to 1,490, and for oxygen-free
high-conductivity copper the classical extrapolation over-predicts the 4~K
reference value by a factor of $3.7\times10^{3}$. The reference-polynomial
curves share one low-temperature mechanism and are grouped accordingly.

Onnes's mercury resistance values~\cite{onnes1911}, digitized from $R(T)$ and
anchored by the reported resistance below $10^{-5}\,\Omega$, show a smooth
normal-state trend followed by a discontinuous drop. Relative to the smooth normal-state and residual-resistance models~\cite{drude1900}, EPOCH localizes a
candidate boundary at 4.20~K, with figure-reading uncertainty and the censored
lower values included in the observation model.

In Millikan's 1916 photoelectric measurement, by contrast, the shape evidence
and the theory evidence disagree~\cite{millikan1916}. Stopping potential is linear in
frequency, $V_{\mathrm{stop}} = (h/e)(\nu - \nu_{0})$, with $R^{2} = 0.996$ and
a slope that recovers $h/e$ to 0.3\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}e), so the four-form
component accepts the shape. On the same four measurements the
theory-conditioned component rejects the classical prediction of
frequency-independent photoelectron energy. Their fitted intercept gives
$\nu_{0} = 4.39\times10^{14}$~Hz, and because all four frequencies lie above
$\nu_{0}$ the output is whole-range incompatibility.

Conversely, independently measured Solar-System orbits recover Kepler's
exponent of 3/2~\cite{kepler1619} to four figures (1.500) and score 0.02, the
four Galilean
moons give exponent 1.500 and score 0.00, and Boyle's original 1662
pressure--volume table~\cite{boyle1662} gives exponent $-0.998$ and scores 0.38
(Supplementary Fig.~1a--c). All three classical controls fall below the 0.5
screening threshold.

\subsection{Advancing the horizon on the same measurements}

We scored each dataset twice on the same grid and under the same error model: first under its incumbent prediction, then under the prediction of the theory that historically replaced it. We call this advancing the horizon. The pre-1900 prediction is rejected in all five cases (bootstrap $p = 0.001$; Methods; Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}a). The successor prediction is not rejected in four of the five: FIRAS under Planck's law~\cite{planck1901}
($p = 1.000$), Bertozzi under relativistic kinetic
energy~\cite{einstein1905relativity} ($p = 0.089$), Onnes under a
superconducting transition model ($p = 1.000$) and Millikan under the
photoelectric relation~\cite{einstein1905photoelectric} ($p = 0.900$), with
discrepancy statistics falling by factors from $2.0\times10^{4}$ to
$7.3\times10^{10}$.

Copper heat capacity, however, is the exception and is sensitive to the error
model. A Debye-plus-electronic form~\cite{debye1912} reduces the discrepancy by
$3.86\times10^{3}$ but remains rejected under a 5\% reference-curve error floor,
the minimum relative error assumed for the reference polynomial ($p = 0.001$), and passes at 7.5\%. Across all five cases, low-dimensional wrong-shape families remain rejected after Benjamini--Hochberg correction for the number of alternatives tested ($q \leq 0.0015$), as do dimensionally coherent wrong-mechanism forms (1,000 bootstrap replicates; $p = q = 1/1001$). These alternatives were chosen after the datasets were known, so this is a retrospective test (Supplementary Note~11). Four of the five discrepancies therefore collapse under the
successful successor prediction and under none of the tested alternatives.

\subsection{Measured series at the same horizon}

To test both components on measured data, we scored sixteen NIST measurement
series (Methods). The series fall into fourteen
mechanism groups, each the set of series produced by one physical mechanism;
five groups have a post-1900 mechanism and nine are classical or metrological, a
metrological group being a calibration artefact or an instrument transfer curve.
Groups are the units over which the AUROC is computed and resampled. For the
five post-1900 groups against the nine others, the learned score gives AUROC
0.62 [0.29, 0.89] and the four-form score 0.78 [0.38, 1.00], and both intervals
include 0.5 (Fig.~\ref{fig:measured} and Supplementary Note~18).

Copper thermal expansion measured from 24~K to 852~K has the highest post-1900
learned score; its coefficient falls towards zero below the Debye temperature,
whereas classical equipartition of lattice modes gives a temperature-independent
coefficient. The density profile of a cold Bose
gas~\cite{bose1924,andersonbec1995} has the highest four-form score. A sulfur
Rydberg series~\cite{rydberg1890}, whose inverse-square form was available
empirically in 1888, belongs to the classical class yet ranks above every
post-1900 group on the learned score (0.1588 against a highest post-1900 group
value of 0.1586; Supplementary Note~18).

Circular-interference transmittance rises to a sharp resonance at 451~nm, the
most unusual shape in the collection, yet has a learned score of 0.0630 and a
four-form score of 1.946, below the four-form threshold (3.391320708)
but above the classical median of 0.566 (Supplementary Note~18). Superconducting
magnetization relaxation (flux creep~\cite{anderson1964fluxcreep}), which no
pre-1900 theory accounts for, is close to logarithmic in time and has the lowest four-form score of any series. Across the whole collection the two
highest learned scores belong to a five-year standard-resistor record and a
23-point polymer-impedance curve.

\subsection{Calibration, thresholds and the observation process}

We compared the four-form component with two baselines on a 36-dataset
synthetic benchmark on which every method receives the same inputs: it reaches
AUROC 0.80 [0.64, 0.94], against 0.58 for Gaussian-process
discrepancy~\cite{rasmussen2006} and 0.77 for
split-conformal prediction (Supplementary Fig.~1d,f). Withholding the classical law, or both the law and the interior regime, leaves AUROC at 1.00 and 0.97 on the benchmark phenomena (Supplementary Note~3).

We fixed the four-form threshold separately at $n = 5$, 9, 43 and 120 on
classical calibration simulations and evaluated it over 12 breakdown and 12
classical families. At $n = 120$, the
true-positive rate is 0.271 [0.073, 0.500] at the 5\% threshold and 0 at
the 1\% quantile, with an observed false-positive rate of 0/96 at both.
Sensitivity is 1.0 for smooth roll-off and discontinuous-drop simulations and
zero for seven families.

We then altered the observation process while holding the underlying classical
relation fixed, to find where that specificity fails. At the strongest simulated
levels a detector calibrated to 5\% reports false-positive rates of 0\% for
calibration drift, 40\% for sensor saturation, 63\% for censoring, 9\% for
x-axis error, 11\% for outliers, 2.5\% for correlated noise and 0\% for
heteroscedasticity (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}b). The output therefore states whether saturation and censoring were tested, and they are tested explicitly for plateau and threshold cases.

We calibrate boundary intervals in the same way. Ordinary measurement bootstrap
intervals cover only 53.9\% of true onsets at a nominal 90\% level (358 development simulations), and 8.5\% for steps. Interval radii calibrated separately at each sample size on 899
independent simulations raise held-out coverage to 91.3\% [89.3\%, 93.0\%]
(Wilson interval) across 900 simulations, with family-wise coverage from 87.8\% for smooth
turnovers to 94.4\% for kinks (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}c). For these simulated cases the reported boundary is therefore an interval.

Finally, simulations extend the evaluation into interiors absent from the
FIRAS and Bertozzi measurements. Blackbody radiation spanning $x = h\nu/kT$ from well
below to well above unity contains a Rayleigh--Jeans interior with
$R^{2} = 0.99$ followed by a departure with split-conformal nonconformity 468 and extrapolation-residual ratio 192. Relativistic kinetic energy yields a localized boundary at $0.86c$, and simulated photoelectric voltage localizes the
frequency threshold.

\section{Discussion}

We have introduced a test of whether a dated body of physical knowledge can
account for a given set of measurements, and applied it at an 1899 horizon. Its
distinguishing feature is that the horizon is an explicit input: training uses
no formula recorded after 1899 and no example of a departure. Trained in this
way, the large encoder ranks 55 twentieth-century law families above nine
held-out pre-1900 groups with AUROC 0.925, against 0.649 for the untrained
network, and 0.857 when the nine controls are regenerated with the 55-family
benchmark's sampling and noise process. The four-form component, which uses no
trained network, reaches 0.888 against 11 held-out pre-1900 families on the
same benchmark. Ranking and detection are different quantities: at the
calibrated 5\% threshold the four-form component detects breakdown datasets at
a true-positive rate of 0.271 [0.073, 0.500] with 0/96 false positives.

EPOCH draws on neural extrapolation~\cite{wang2023discovery,xu2021extrapolate},
split-conformal prediction, Bayesian model discrepancy and change-point
detection~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal,page1954cusum,kennedy2001bayesian}, and
shares machinery with model-independent searches in particle
physics\cite{dagnolo2019newphysics} and with machine rediscovery of natural
laws~\cite{guimera2020bayesian,udrescu2020feynman,schmidt2009distilling}. What
differs is the target of inference: we test the predictive domain of a stated model before asking symbolic regression or theory construction for a
replacement. Once a calibrated discrepancy exists, that search can proceed under
a parsimony prior~\cite{he2026searchfree}, as the Bayesian machine scientist and
AI Feynman do in recovering Planck-type expressions from blackbody-type
data~\cite{guimera2020bayesian,udrescu2020feynman}, and physical interpretation
then supplies the mechanism behind the fitted form.

A discrepancy is interpretable only when it is tied to a named incumbent. When
we score each historical dataset twice on the same grid and under the same
error model, four of five old-horizon discrepancies disappear once the successor prediction is substituted, while wrong-shape and dimensionally
coherent wrong-mechanism alternatives stay rejected after multiplicity
correction. Copper heat capacity is the exception: the successor model passes only when the reference-curve error floor is relaxed from 5\% to 7.5\%, so the error floor has to be stated with the incumbent prediction. For a closed-form theory, advancing
the horizon amounts to replacing the incumbent prediction and recalibrating its
null distribution, and simulator-based references give the corresponding
construction where no closed form exists. A conclusion about a named physical
theory requires that the admissible laws and the observation model
encode that theory's prediction for the measured quantity (Methods).

Shape novelty and predictive failure are not the same quantity. Later affine,
power and exponential laws often stay close to one of the four generic forms
(learned and four-form AUROC 0.629/0.795). Threshold and piecewise relations
separate on the four-form score (0.982) but not on the learned score (0.600),
and nonmonotone and oscillatory relations separate on both (0.909 and 0.864).
Conversely, a discrepancy with a named incumbent can keep an ordinary shape, as
Millikan's stopping potential and the Pound--Rebka redshift both do. A classical
circular-interference resonance scores 0.0630 on the learned component and
stays below the four-form threshold, while superconducting flux
creep~\cite{anderson1964fluxcreep}, which no pre-1900 theory accounts for, is
close to logarithmic in time and sits at the bottom of the four-form ranking.
Keeping the component scores separate is what preserves this distinction
between an unusual shape and the failure of a named prediction, and we expect
it to matter most where the departure keeps an ordinary functional form.

The negative class of the 1899 experiment is small. Nine held-out pre-1900
groups carry the primary learned comparison and set the width of its interval,
and fourteen mechanism groups set the width of the measured-series intervals.
The learned ranking also depends on how the later formulas were generated: the
regenerated-control value of 0.857, from an analysis added after the primary
results were known, controls for sampling within the 55-family benchmark only, and when the later formulas are drawn by the same generator as
the training corpus the trained model gives 0.536 [0.424, 0.643] (Results),
near chance. On measured series the ranking falls to 0.62 for the learned
component and 0.78 for the four-form component, with intervals that span
chance. The two highest learned scores in that collection belong to a
standard-resistor record and a polymer-impedance curve, a pattern consistent with the observation process setting the largest learned-score differences on measured data. Whether this is the sensitivity that saturation and censoring expose for the four-form component in the simulated instrument tests remains to be tested.

Large libraries of measured relations could be screened against a fixed horizon, and selected cases rerun with explicit measurement errors and incumbent
predictions to obtain a model test and, where an interior is sampled, a boundary estimate. Moving the horizon to another period requires a different dated
corpus, admitted prediction, observation model and calibration set. The next
experiment we plan is to fix the full decision procedure in advance and run it against measurements selected without regard to whether their physics is pre- or post-1900, and whose instrument models have been characterized.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Terms}

\textit{Knowledge horizon}: a calendar date; only physical relations recorded
before it are admitted to the training corpus and to the laws a test may fit;
here 1899. \textit{Incumbent}: the pre-horizon law that a test examines for a
given dataset; the incumbent prediction is its numerical prediction for the
measured quantity. \textit{Interior} and \textit{frontier}: the contiguous
stretch of a dataset over which one admitted pre-horizon form fits well, and the
remainder, on which the extrapolation of that fit is tested. \textit{Point
cloud}: the $(x, y)$ pairs of one relation, normalized to remove scale and
stripped of formula, units, labels and ordering; the only input the learned and
four-form components receive. \textit{Law family} and \textit{mechanism group}:
the set of point clouds derived from one physical law traced to one dated
source, and the set of measured series produced by one physical mechanism; both
are the units over which AUROCs are computed and bootstrap intervals resampled.

\textit{Four-form component}: fits the best of power, exponential, linear and
constant forms on the interior, extrapolates to the frontier and reports the
split-conformal nonconformity (the mean absolute frontier residual divided by
the 90th percentile of the interior absolute residuals) and the
extrapolation-residual ratio (the median absolute log-residual at the frontier
divided by its interior value), each with a floor in its denominator; called
the data-anchored or statistical component in the code and SI.
\textit{Theory-conditioned component}: computes the residuals of the
measurements from the incumbent prediction, scaled by the stated measurement errors, and compares their size with a null distribution simulated from that
prediction on the same grid with the same errors. \textit{Learned component}: a
neural encoder trained only on point clouds from pre-horizon relations, with no
example of a failure; its score is the distance of a new cloud's
embedding from those of the training relations; the large encoder has 20.9
million parameters and the compact encoder has width 128 and uses no unit
information. \textit{Null distribution}: the distribution of a discrepancy
statistic over many datasets simulated from the incumbent prediction on the same
$x$-grid and with the same error model as the real measurements.
\textit{Observation model}: the stated description of how the measurements
were produced, comprising error distribution, censoring or saturation, and grid;
an error floor is a minimum relative error assigned when no published covariance
exists.

All thresholds, weights and scoring rules were fixed, and their files
time-stamped and hashed, before any test data were scored; which component
evaluates a dataset is decided by a rule fixed before scoring. \textit{Verdicts}:
compatible (the incumbent fits over the measured range); localized incompatibility (the incumbent fits an interior and fails beyond an estimated boundary); whole-range incompatibility (the incumbent fails
everywhere sampled); abstention (the inputs do not support a test).
\textit{Advancing the horizon}: rescoring the same measurements on the same
grid and with the same error model after replacing the incumbent prediction by
the prediction of the theory that historically superseded it.
\textit{Regenerated} and \textit{original controls}: the nine pre-1900 control
groups regenerated with the sampling and noise process of the later families,
and the same groups as originally generated; the same-generator set is a set of
later formulas generated by the same process as the pre-1900 corpus.
\textit{Untrained network}: the same network with its initial random weights
and no training steps.

\subsection{Specifying a test}

An EPOCH test specifies
$\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{M},\Theta,P_\varepsilon,I,A,S,\alpha)$. Here
$\mathcal{M}$ lists the admissible models, $\Theta$ the fitted parameters,
$P_\varepsilon$ specifies the observation and censoring errors, $I$ identifies
the fitting data, $A$ specifies every adaptive choice, $S$ is the discrepancy
statistic and $\alpha$ is the error rate. Because direction choice, window
search, model selection and parameter fitting all belong to $A$, each of them is
rerun inside every calibration and null replicate rather than being fixed once
on the test data. Confirmatory tests use the finite-sample upper-tail
probability $p=(1+\#\{S_i\geq S_*\})/(n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1)$ with ties counted
conservatively, and dataset scans report Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted
$q$-values~\cite{benjamini1995fdr}. The main reported quantity is the true-positive rate at a threshold targeted to 5\% false positives on independent calibration,
with the 1\% threshold secondary; the 0.5 threshold used in the historical screen, the median calibration score, selects cases for further testing. The finite-sample guarantee of the upper-tail
test holds when calibration and test units are exchangeable; that unit is the
law family (the source-law family of the SI) or the mechanism group, not the
individual point or frontier.

\subsection{The four forms and the interior and frontier}

The four-form component draws on a fixed vocabulary of power, exponential,
linear and constant forms. We tested and discarded an eight-form ablation
adding quadratic, cubic, square-root and logarithmic terms, because the
four-form version extrapolates better on validation data. Given a point cloud, a fixed rule takes
the candidate interior to be the contiguous regime in which one admitted form
fits with high interior $R^{2}$, and the frontier to be the complementary
region. The search runs from both ends and fit quality decides whether the low-
or the high-value direction is retained. The split-conformal
nonconformity~\cite{vovk2005} is the mean absolute frontier residual divided by
the 90th percentile of the interior absolute residuals, and the
extrapolation-residual ratio
compares the median log-residual at the frontier with its interior value. Each
form is fitted from several initializations, and each denominator carries a
floor, one percent of the median interior response magnitude for the
nonconformity and 0.01 for the median interior log-residual, so that a
near-perfect interior fit cannot produce an unbounded ratio. Relations with fewer than
20 points are calibrated against size-matched simulations.

\subsection{Large-scale pre-1900 representation}

The scaling experiment uses a 20,908,547-parameter encoder trained alongside a
second, training-only network, without any anomaly supervision. Its query branch
is a permutation-invariant six-block Set Transformer\cite{lee2019settransformer}
of width 384 with eight heads, four pooling tokens and a 256-dimensional
normalized output. During pretraining it is aligned to that
expression-tree network, built from four edge-aware graph updates and two
graph-local attention layers, one of whose update networks is a 96-channel
eight-basis radial-basis-function Kolmogorov--Arnold network
module~\cite{liu2025kan}. At query time the encoder receives an unordered
$(x,y)$ cloud normalized by median and interquartile range, and nothing else: no
equation, graph, units, date, law name, post-cutoff label or deformation
example.

Training draws on 880,896 clouds derived from 3,441 dimensionless relations $y = f(x)$, each a reduction of a pre-1900 law. These are grouped into 91 sets of clouds
treated as the same law during training, spanning 64 law families.
Held-out calibration and pre-1900 test sets contain 11,648 and 62,336 clouds from 91
and 487 formulas. Grouping the test-set formulas by mechanism gives the nine pre-1900 control groups used for every AUROC reported here (the historical-family groups of the SI). The model trains for 30 epochs at batch size 1,024, and the weights used for scoring are those selected by a rule fixed before any later-physics evaluation. Its learned score averages four calibrated
ranks, each transformed by its own pre-1900 calibration empirical cumulative
distribution function: top-eight cosine distance to graph-formula embeddings,
top-eight distance to identity centroids, top-eight distance to point-formula
prototypes, and shrinkage-Mahalanobis
distance~\cite{mahalanobis1936,ledoit2004} to those prototypes. Scores are summarized by the median over clouds within a formula and then within a law family or control group, and family- and group-level AUROCs use 2,000 grouped-bootstrap
replicates; unless stated otherwise, bracketed intervals in this paper are 95\%
bootstrap intervals resampled at that level. The untrained network is scored by the identical procedure. 

To build the regenerated controls we regenerated all nine pre-1900 control groups with the 55-family benchmark's point count, x-jitter, repeat count,
noise range and heteroscedastic profile, leaving the trained weights, the stored training embeddings, the calibration distribution function, score and aggregation unchanged.
We then repeated the same analysis with the untrained network. A separate
96,064-cloud set of later formulas generated by the same process as the
pre-1900 corpus, not taken from the 55-family benchmark, and a pilot with a 1950 horizon, are reported in Supplementary Note~16.

\subsection{Compact encoder and its controls}

The compact encoder is a set transformer of width 128 with three
induced-set-attention blocks, and trains for 1,000 steps on two independently subsampled and rescaled copies of each of 205 pre-1900 point clouds from 40 families. Families are
sampled without replacement so that repeated laws cannot become false
contrastive negatives, and an auxiliary head predicts functional signatures. No
breakdown, saturation, step, kink, splice or crossover example enters this
optimization. We trained matched models with SI dimensions and with zeroed
dimension metadata; the model trained without unit information is the one reported for the synthetic benchmark. The k-nearest-neighbour and shrinkage-Mahalanobis scores are compared against a matched random encoder. Leave-family-out calibration
holds out each of the 40 training families in full, takes the median over the eight resampled copies of each dataset and then over the datasets within a family. The
threshold, the 95th percentile of the 40 held-out family scores, is fixed before evaluation.

The generic-function control encoder is a second encoder trained without unit information that shares
architecture, seed, batch size, augmentation, auxiliary objective and 1,000-step
schedule but is trained on 205 point clouds
from globally valid smooth expressions in 40 pseudo-families, matched to the
historical set in dataset count, family-size profile and the histogram of eight-bit functional signatures, each bit recording whether one elementary function type (exponential, logarithm, sine, square root, power, inverse power, polynomial terms or hyperbolic) appears in the formula. We retrained both encoders from scratch under
three paired initialization and augmentation seeds while the resampling seed and the 24 breakdown and classical families stay fixed, and we report the
observed range and sample standard deviation across those runs. Finally, each of 46 held-out point clouds is paired
with saturation, step, kink, splice, roll-off and crossover deformations that
retain its physical-unit exponents. Each of seven law families contributes one undeformed and six deformed scores, the AUROC
difference is bootstrapped by family, and each encoder is compared with its own threshold, the 95th percentile of its held-out family scores. Thirty-four of those datasets carry at least one nonzero dimensional exponent.

Which component scores a point cloud is fixed in advance. Point clouds with at
least 20 observations receive a learned score, point clouds whose response is
nearly constant are sent instead to the theory-conditioned component, which
tests a constant against noise, and formula and deformation families are the
resampling units throughout.

\subsection{Post-1900 formula benchmark}

The 55-family benchmark contains 55 law families
first valid between 1901 and 1996, each carrying a primary historical citation
and 45 of them a DOI. The families include thermionic
emission~\cite{richardson1901}, discrete atomic excitation~\cite{franck1914},
parity violation~\cite{wu1957}, absence of diffusion in disordered
lattices~\cite{anderson1958}, the local-hidden-variable bound and its
experimental test~\cite{bell1964,aspect1982} and the first gravitational-wave
detection~\cite{abbott2016}. Every law is represented by a one-dimensional form $y = f(x)$ in dimensionless variables over a fixed
support, sampled eight times at 120 points with varying nuisance parameters,
sampling and 0.5--2.5\% noise, giving 440 datasets across 55 families. Special
reductions, such as the BCS interpolation~\cite{bardeen1957}, the two-flavour
neutrino survival curve~\cite{maki1962} and the ideal quantum-Hall
staircase~\cite{klitzing1980}, are noted in the benchmark listing. No benchmark family overlaps a family of the pre-1900 corpus.

The compact encoder, its calibration and its thresholds are those fixed in the
leave-family-out evaluation above. The learned score is summarized by the median over resampled copies, datasets and families and compared with the threshold fixed on the 40 held-out family scores. The four-form score reruns the full four-form selection and
extrapolation procedure against the existing $n = 120$ thresholds. Eleven cited
pre-1900 families are the controls, AUROC intervals resample families, and
twelve of the post-1900 laws deliberately overlap the affine, power or exponential forms. Component union and intersection rates are reported in Supplementary Note~15, and per-family conformal $p$-values are in the data package. The 11 further twentieth-century cases were assembled after the primary scores were known (Supplementary Note~17).

\subsection{Historical measurements and horizon-specificity controls}

We analyse the published COBE-FIRAS monopole spectrum with its pointwise errors,
five Bertozzi values digitized from the 1964 report, NIST specific-heat reference
polynomials for six materials, Onnes resistance values including upper limits,
Millikan sodium stopping potentials and Solar-System orbital constants. The incumbent predictions are the classical predictions that follow from physics available before 1900; where the standard statement of a prediction was published later, as for the Rayleigh--Jeans form in 1900 and 1905 and the Drude model in 1900, we cite that statement as the reference and do not use it as a training source. For each case we record the model set, fitted parameters, observation model, fit subset, adaptive choices, decision rule and output. We evaluate old- and advanced-horizon
null distributions on the same $x$-grid; explicit error floors stand in for
unavailable covariance and for digitization uncertainty, and we vary them in
sensitivity analysis.

To establish that a collapse in the discrepancy is specific to the successful
successor,
wrong-shape and dimensionally coherent wrong-mechanism forms are refitted on the
same case grids and observation models with 1,000 parametric-bootstrap
replicates. The physical controls substitute fermionic occupation for photon
statistics, a proton for the electron rest-energy scale, an Einstein oscillator~\cite{einstein1907solid}
for Debye acoustic modes~\cite{debye1912}, Arrhenius transport for the superconducting transition,
and Compton recoil for the photoelectric slope.

\subsection{Measured series}

We took sixteen series unmodified from the NIST Dataplot and Statistical
Reference Dataset collections. For each series we specified, before any score
was computed, the data columns, the 1899 incumbent, the mechanism group and
whether the mechanism was known before 1899. Where a file header and its
stored block disagree on column order, the order is resolved against certified
values, and a file that is a subset or decimation of another series shares that
series' mechanism group. Series are sorted by abscissa and thinned uniformly to
at most 2,000 points. The learned component applies the fixed rule that sends
nearly constant responses to the theory-conditioned component, rescales each
measured series and adds no synthetic noise, with the stored training embeddings computed from the pre-1900 training set. The four-form component reruns the
complete four-form direction, window, model-selection and scoring procedure on
eight resampled copies of each series at the largest calibration sample size the series supports.
Scores are summarized by the median within a series and then within a mechanism group, and
the grouped AUROC resamples mechanism groups.

\subsection{Synthetic suites, baselines and localization}

The held-out evaluation comprises 180 labelled datasets and the fair baseline
suite 36, on which split-conformal prediction, the extrapolation-residual ratio,
Gaussian-process discrepancy~\cite{rasmussen2006}, CUSUM~\cite{page1954cusum}
and RESET~\cite{ramsey1969} are compared. The mechanism-level benchmark contains
12 classical and 12 breakdown families with eight datasets each; its 5\% and 1\%
thresholds come from 785 valid size-matched calibration simulations, and
uncertainty is resampled by breakdown or classical family. The learned-component evaluation uses 192 datasets over the same 24 families. Instrument tests apply
calibration drift, sensor saturation, censoring, x-axis error, outliers,
correlated noise and heteroscedasticity to classical synthetic relations while
leaving the underlying law intact, and boundary intervals are obtained by
split-conformal calibration of the normalized localization error separately at
$n = 40$, 80 and 160.

\subsection{Measured-series collection}

We downloaded forty-four files from the NIST Dataplot and Statistical Reference
Dataset collections. The collection holds 20
instrument controls, 11 classical controls, six controls whose post-1900
mechanism is known and seven horizon cases. The sixteen series analysed are listed in Supplementary Note~18.
